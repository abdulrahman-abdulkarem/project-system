# Taste Library

The reference bank every project's design direction is drawn from. Steps 2–4 of the design workflow say "pick the family" and "give 2–3 references" — this is what they point at.

**Why it exists.** You can't brief "make it look good." You *can* hand over concrete examples of good, which turns an unanswerable instruction into a matchable target. It's also the only part of the whole system that is yours — rules and checkpoints can be copied from anyone; a library of things you personally react to can't.

**Target:** 15–30 entries across 3–4 families. Built once over a few sessions, then grown a couple of entries at a time, forever.

---

# BUILD GUIDE — start here

Four sessions. Do them in order; the order matters, because judging while you collect is what kills momentum.

---

## Session 1 — Hunt and capture (45 minutes, no judging)

The only rule this session: **collect, don't analyse.** If you stop to work out *why* you like something you'll get four entries and give up. Aim for 20–30 captures.

**1. Set up the folder.** Screenshots go in `screenshots/` next to this file. Name them `sitename-page.png` — `careem-home.png`, `airbnb-search.png`, `linear-issues.png`.

**2. Capture properly — full page, not the visible window.** In Chrome: `Ctrl+Shift+P` → type "screenshot" → **Capture full size screenshot**. The scroll rhythm and section transitions are half of what you're collecting, and a viewport crop loses them.

**3. Grab TWO pages per site, and make the second one an interior page.**

This is the most important instruction here. A homepage is a marketing artifact — big type, hero, persuasion. The **list page, detail page, or form** is where the real design system lives: how cards are built, how dense information is organised, how filters and states are handled. That interior page is what you actually need for ArabGo-shaped work, and it's the page everyone forgets to save.

**4. Where to hunt.**

*General galleries:* Land-book, SiteInspire, Httpster, Minimal Gallery, Godly. Awwwards is worth a look but skews heavily toward expressive brand sites — treat it as Family 4 material, not product design.

*Product and app UI:* Mobbin is the strongest source for Family 2 (clean utility) — real screens from real apps, including flows and states.

*Arabic and Gulf/MENA — hunt these deliberately, no gallery will surface them for you:* Careem, Talabat, Noon, Jahez, Nana, Tamara, Tabby, Salla, Zid, Foodics, Bayut, Mrsool, Haraj. Saudi government digital services (Absher, Tawakkalna) are genuinely well-designed and worth studying for Arabic form and data patterns. For Arabic editorial type: Al Jazeera, Asharq. *(Verify these are still good examples when you look — I'm going from knowledge, not from viewing them.)*

*Your own browsing:* anything that stopped your scroll this month. That's the highest-quality source you have.

**5. What NOT to collect.** Dribbble shots and concept mockups that aren't shipped products. They're illustrations *of* UI — no real content, no edge cases, no long names, no empty states, no error messages. They'll teach you a look that falls apart the moment real data hits it.

**6. The only filter this session:** did you actually react to it? Not "is it well-regarded." If you're neutral, skip it. A library of things you're neutral about is worse than a small library of things you love.

---

## Session 2 — Sort into families (30 minutes)

**1. Look at everything at once.** Open the screenshots folder in a grid view — Windows Explorer, large thumbnails. Seeing them together is what makes the groupings obvious.

**2. Group by *feel*, not by industry.** Two directories can belong to different families; a directory and a bank can belong to the same one.

**3. Let the families emerge.** I've seeded four below as starting points, but if your captures cluster differently, rename them. Yours should be the real ones.

**4. Notice the imbalance.** If 20 of 25 land in one family, that's useful information about your taste — and a signal to go hunt deliberately in the thin ones, because a single-family library can only ever brief one look.

**5. Drop each entry in under its family** with just the name, URL, and screenshot filename. Leave the vocabulary blank.

---

## Session 3 — Decode into vocabulary (do this with me)

This is the hard part, and it's where a library usually dies — you know you like something and can't say why. Don't sit alone trying to force it.

**Send me the screenshots here, three or four at a time**, and say roughly what drew you to each — even if it's just "this feels calm" or "I like how heavy the headline is."

I'll decode each into mechanical vocabulary: the ground colour, the type pairing and scale, the shadow character, the spacing rhythm, the single accent, the border treatment, the corner radius language. Those are the words that paste into a prompt.

Then **you correct me.** If I say "warm paper ground" and it doesn't feel warm to you, say so — the library has to encode your reaction, not my description. That correction step is what makes it yours.

**Fill in for each entry:**
- **Vocabulary** — 3–6 mechanical keywords
- **What makes it work** — 1–2 sentences, specific and mechanical, not appreciative
- **What I'd steal** — the one concrete thing you'd actually reuse

That last field does the most work. "I'd steal the way the card border is 1px warm-grey instead of a shadow" is reusable. "I like the vibe" isn't.

---

## Session 4 — Test it (20 minutes)

A library you haven't used is a guess. Test it before trusting it.

**1. Pick a real brief.** ArabGo's provider card, or a page from a project you're planning.

**2. Fill in the 4-Part Prompt** at the bottom of this file, pulling the aesthetic and vocabulary straight from your entries.

**3. Run it** — through impeccable, or Claude Code, or here.

**4. Judge the output against one question:** does it look like the family you picked, or does it look generic? If generic, the vocabulary was too vague — go back and make it more mechanical. That's the failure mode, and it's fixable.

---

## Ongoing — the habit

Add one or two entries whenever something stops your scroll. Full-page screenshot, URL, three keywords while the reaction is fresh — two minutes. A library that grows a little every month beats one built in a weekend and never touched.

Every six months or so, delete the entries you no longer react to. Taste moves.

### Careem
- **URL:** careem.com
- **Screenshot:** screenshots/careem/ (22 captures, AR + EN — home, service page, form, FAQ,
  blog index, footer)
- **Family:** NOT warm premium marketplace — bold product surface. Near-white ground, zero
  shadows, flat saturated accents. Reassign when Family 2 or a new family is defined.
- **Script/direction:** Bilingual — the most valuable reference in this library
- **Vocabulary:** near-white ground · zero elevation · signal colour reserved for action ·
  three-tier radius · one icon set · notched gradient dividers
- **What makes it work:**
  Separation is done entirely by ground colour and corner radius — there is not a single
  shadow on the site. One bright green is reserved exclusively for primary buttons and
  never used decoratively, so every call to action is unmissable without any other emphasis.
- **What I'd steal:**
  Four containers instead of eighteen cards. ~18 services are organised as four coloured
  cards, each holding a plain text list of what's inside it — no icon per service, no tile
  per service. The default answer to "many categories" is a grid of identical cards; this
  is better, and it scales.

**Neutral — observed, not adopted:** the four card colours (indigo / mint / violet / navy)
have no harmonic relationship. Confident rather than messy, but not a device to reuse.

**Liked:** the notched gradient section dividers — angled bites cut from the top and bottom
edges instead of straight horizontal rules. Reads as brand rather than decoration.

#### Careem — RTL findings (cross-reference: RTL / Arabic pattern notes)
- The Arabic wordmark is set **larger** than the Latin one in the same nav bar. Arabic has no
  ascenders or descenders, so at equal nominal size it reads smaller. They corrected for it.
- Latin product names are never transliterated: "Careem Plus" becomes "كريم Plus",
  "Quik Electronics" becomes "Quik إلكترونيات". The descriptor translates; the name doesn't.
- **The English two-weight headline device is dropped in Arabic.** English gets a heavy word
  over a medium word on two lines ("**Go** / anywhere"); Arabic gets one word, one weight
  ("رحلاتك"). They let the device go rather than forcing it. This is the single most
  instructive thing in the capture set: a typographic device that works in one script may
  have no equivalent in the other, and forcing it is worse than dropping it.
- Mirroring is complete: nav order, logo side, language switcher side, card order, card arrow
  direction, footer column order. Nothing left stranded.
- Numbers, percentages and country codes keep Latin order inside Arabic text.
- **Live bug, worth adding to `lang check`:** in the Arabic blog cards a sentence-ending
  period renders at the *start* of the line. A bidi punctuation failure that only appears in
  Arabic and that no English-language QA pass would catch.

### noon
- **URL:** noon.com
- **Screenshot:** screenshots/noon/ (AR + EN — home, category grid, listing with filters,
  product detail, cart, account)
- **Family:** Dense transactional marketplace. Not warm premium either — that seeded family
  now has zero real members and probably needs renaming.
- **Script/direction:** Bilingual
- **Vocabulary:** pale grey ground · zero elevation · chrome-only brand colour ·
  tight 8px radius · one bold element per card · strict colour jobs
- **What makes it work:**
  Colour has enforced jobs and never drifts: yellow is chrome only (the header bar, never a
  button), blue is the primary action, green appears only on discount percentages and the
  word FREE. Within a single card, exactly one element is bold — the live price — so nine
  layers of information still have a reading order.
- **What I'd steal:**
  The grouped category grid. Categories are presented as labelled rows (Women's fashion,
  Beauty, Kitchen & home), seven photo tiles per row, real product photography on a soft
  tinted ground rather than icons, bold label beneath each tile, one "View all" per row.
  The row grouping is what keeps it readable — it is the only part of noon where the grid
  has rhythm instead of mass.

**Neutral — observed, not adopted:** the nine-layer product card. One card is readable; a
grid of twenty is a mess, because every card shouts the same nine things and nothing
recedes. **Density is a property of the grid, not of the card.** That is the finding, and
it is the reason the card itself is not a steal.

**Direction decision (mine, not noon's):** noon confines its brand colour to the chrome and
leaves the content neutral; Careem carries green through the whole page. For ArabGo I prefer
Careem's approach — brand colour present throughout, not just in the header.

#### noon — ANTI-REFERENCE: the homepage
Promo band, hero carousel, category rail, "More reasons to shop", "Mega deals" with a
countdown, "In focus", bestsellers, savings codes, "Lowest prices", Back to School — every
block at equal visual volume with no reading order. This is what a marketplace homepage
becomes when every category has bought placement. Avoid for ArabGo's home.

#### noon — RTL findings
- **The price row mirrors, but the number stays glued.** Currency symbol plus numeral behave
  as one LTR island; the row order reverses around it, so the struck-through original price
  sits to the LEFT of the live price in Arabic and to the right in English.
- **The rating flips its anchor:** `★ 4.5 (1.2K)` becomes `(1.2K) 4.5 ★` — the star moves to
  the right so it still leads the reading order.
- The filter sidebar fully mirrors (right in Arabic, left in English), checkboxes on the
  outer edge in both.
- **Mixed-script titles wrap badly** — one Arabic word followed by a long Latin product
  string, truncated mid-phrase. Careem avoided this by keeping Latin to short brand names;
  noon can't, because its catalogue is full of long English names. **This is ArabGo's exact
  problem: Arabic service names beside Latin brand names inside a constrained card.**

#### Cross-site observation (Careem + noon)
Neither site uses a single shadow. Both separate elements by ground colour, hairline borders
and corner radius alone. Two out of two Gulf sites — watch whether this holds as a regional
convention or is a coincidence of these two.

# FAMILY 2 — Clean utility / product surface

Dashboards, tools, admin panels — anything where someone is completing a task, not being persuaded. Restrained palette, tight type scale, density without clutter, near-zero decoration, motion only for state feedback.

*Empty — target 3–5 entries. Start with tools you actually enjoy using. Mobbin is the best source.*

---

# FAMILY 3 — Editorial / content-forward

Reading-led design. Strong type hierarchy, deliberate measure, generous line-height, imagery serving the text. Relevant whenever content is the product.

*Empty — target 3–5 entries. Hunt Arabic editorial specifically; Arabic type at reading sizes behaves differently from Latin and almost nothing in Western galleries will teach you this.*

---

# FAMILY 4 — Bold / expressive brand surface

Launch pages, studios, portfolios — where being memorable *is* the job. The expressive scroll techniques from the motion checkpoint are legitimate here. Kept separate so its choices never leak into product surfaces.

*Empty — target 3–5 entries. Awwwards is the right hunting ground for this one and only this one.*

# ANTI-REFERENCES

What you've explicitly rejected, and why. Writing the reason down stops the same mistake returning under a new name.

### ArabGo redesign, July 2026
- **Why rejected:** Too generic; lost the project's identity.
- **The lesson:** Genericness is what you get when no direction was decided — not a failure of execution.

### "AI slop" tells — avoid across every family
- Blue-to-purple gradients
- Inter everywhere, every weight, for everything
- The same three-card layout regardless of content
- Emoji standing in for icons
- Evenly-distributed rainbow palettes with no hierarchy
- Decorative floating blobs with no structural purpose *(measured on ArabGo: a real main-thread cost as well as a visual one)*

# RTL / Arabic pattern notes

Cross-cutting, not a family — an Arabic site can belong to any family above. This collects what you observe about RTL execution specifically, because no Western gallery will teach it.

Record as you find examples:
- How Arabic display type is sized and weighted against Latin in the same layout
- Which Arabic faces hold up at large display sizes, not just at body sizes
- How navigation, search and filter patterns mirror in practice
- What a good bilingual language switcher looks like
- Where mixed-direction content (prices, phone numbers, brand names) is handled well

*Empty — fill as you collect.*

# The 4-Part Prompt

What this library feeds. Give all four parts, every time.

```
AESTHETIC
Family: [family name from this library]
Vocabulary: [keywords from the 2–3 entries you picked]

REFERENCE
[2–3 specific entries — screenshots attached, URLs listed]
Match the FEEL, never copy the layout.

INTENT
What it is: [product]
Who it's for: [audience]
Primary action: [the one thing they should do]
Register: [product surface or brand surface]
Direction: [LTR / RTL / bilingual]

GUARDRAILS
ALWAYS: [brand colours, one deliberate accent, real contrast, correct reading direction, one icon set]
NEVER: [blue-purple gradients, Inter everywhere, emoji as icons, identical card grids, rainbow palettes,
        Latin typographic treatments on Arabic]
```

Then: cast wide (a few distinct directions side by side) → pick → 2–3 variants of the winner → tweak in small moves. Never one-shot.
